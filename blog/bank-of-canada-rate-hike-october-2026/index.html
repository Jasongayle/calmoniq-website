The Bank of Canada just raised its policy rate to 2.5%. Here's what it means for your money.

October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

On October 28, 2026, the Bank of Canada raised its policy interest rate by 25 basis points (a quarter of a percentage point) to 2.5%. It is the first rate increase since 2023, and the Bank made the move to stop high energy prices from pushing up the cost of everything else. The next rate decision is December 9. If you are a regular Canadian with a mortgage, a line of credit, some debt, or savings, here is what a higher policy rate actually means, in plain language.

The quick version: variable borrowing (variable mortgages, HELOCs, lines of credit) gets a little more expensive right away. Fixed loans do not change until you renew. Savings rates may tick up slightly. The best move is to know your numbers and have a plan.

First, what is the "policy rate"?

The policy rate (also called the overnight rate) is the interest rate the Bank of Canada sets for banks borrowing from each other overnight. It sounds far from your wallet, but it is the starting point for your bank's prime rate. When the policy rate goes up, prime usually follows by the same amount within days. And prime is what sets the interest on a lot of everyday borrowing.

If you have a variable-rate mortgage or a HELOC

These are tied directly to prime, so they move almost immediately:

  • Adjustable-rate mortgage: your monthly payment goes up.
  • Variable-rate mortgage with a fixed payment: your payment stays the same, but more of it goes to interest and less to principal, so you pay the home off more slowly.

Either way, a HELOC or unsecured line of credit now costs a bit more to carry each month.

If you have a fixed-rate mortgage

Short-term good news: nothing changes right now. Your rate is locked for your term. The thing to watch is your renewal date. If you renew in the next year or two, you could be renewing at a higher rate than you have today, which can mean a noticeably bigger payment. It is worth knowing that number before it arrives, not after.

What it means for credit cards and other debt

Most credit cards charge a flat, high rate (often around 19.99%) that does not move with the Bank of Canada. But a rate hike is a good reminder that this is the most expensive money you can borrow. When rates are climbing, paying down high-interest debt is one of the best guaranteed returns you can get.

Is there any upside? Savings.

A little. When rates rise, high-interest savings accounts and GICs often pay a bit more. If you keep an emergency fund or some cash set aside, it may earn slightly more. Shop around, because the big banks are usually not the best payers.

Four things worth doing this week

  1. Know your numbers. Write down each debt: the balance, the interest rate, and (for your mortgage) your renewal date. You cannot plan around numbers you cannot see.
  2. Stress-test your renewal. If your mortgage renews soon, work out what the payment looks like at a higher rate. A small jump in rate can be a real jump in payment.
  3. Attack the highest-interest debt first. List your debts by rate and throw extra money at the top of the list (the "avalanche" method). It saves the most interest.
  4. Recheck your budget. Higher borrowing costs mean less room elsewhere. Knowing how much is actually safe to spend each month keeps a rate change from quietly draining your account.

The bottom line

A 25-basis-point hike is not a crisis, but it is a nudge. If your borrowing is variable, it costs a little more today. If it is fixed, the real question is what you renew into later.
